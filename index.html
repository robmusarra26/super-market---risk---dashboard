How to read it: 0–2.9 = Low • 3.0–4.4 = Guarded • 4.5–5.9 = Elevated • 6.0–7.4 = High • 7.5–10 = Very high. The score is a monitoring gauge, not a forecast of market losses.
Score methodology
The risk score now uses only objective, automatically refreshed data. No part of the score depends on you guessing whether earnings or market breadth are strong or weak.
Included: US 10-year yield, credit spreads, core inflation, jobless claims, yield curve, VIX and oil.
Not scored: corporate earnings and market breadth until we can source them reliably and automatically.

Tier 1 — main decision signals
These carry the most weight in the dashboard because they tell us about the cost of money, corporate stress and whether profits are still supporting share prices.
US 10-year yield
The cost of long-term borrowing. Rising yields can pressure shares, property and bond prices. For your super: a sustained rise can be harder on growth-heavy options and can also temporarily reduce fixed-interest values; stabilising or falling yields usually improve the backdrop for bonds.

High-yield credit spread
The extra interest riskier companies must pay over government bonds. Rising spreads mean investors are becoming more worried about company debt. For your super: widening spreads usually increase risk for share-heavy portfolios; calm or falling spreads suggest companies are coping better.

Corporate earnings
Company profit trends are important for shares, but this dashboard does not score them until a dependable automatic source is available. For your super: weakening earnings would normally make growth-heavy options more vulnerable.

Tier 2 — early warning / confirmation
These help explain why the main signals are moving and whether recession or inflation risk is strengthening.
Core PCE inflation
A measure of underlying US inflation. Persistent inflation can keep interest rates and bond yields higher for longer. For your super: that can pressure both shares and fixed interest in the short term; easing inflation is generally more supportive of bonds and diversified growth assets.
—
—
Initial jobless claims
An early read on the US labour market. A sustained rise can signal that the economy is starting to weaken. For your super: rising claims can increase recession risk, which is usually negative for shares but can become supportive of high-quality fixed interest if yields fall.
—
—
10Y–2Y yield curve
Compares long- and short-term government yields. It helps show what the bond market expects for growth and future interest rates. For your super: a changing curve can warn that the economic cycle is shifting; it matters most when combined with weakening jobs, credit stress and earnings.

Tier 3 — supporting signals
These are useful confirmation signals. They matter, but they should not drive a portfolio decision by themselves.
VIX fear gauge
A measure of expected share-market volatility. A sharp rise usually means investors are becoming more nervous. For your super: a sharp VIX rise often means more short-term volatility in growth assets, but it is better used as confirmation than as a stand-alone signal.
—
—
WTI oil
Oil can amplify inflation and economic shocks. A rapid rise can make it harder for central banks to cut rates. For your super: a large oil spike can hurt both shares and bonds if it pushes inflation higher, while falling oil can reduce inflation pressure.
—
—
Market breadth
Breadth shows whether a rally is supported by many companies or only a few large stocks. It remains useful context, but is not scored until we add an automatic source. For your super: narrow breadth can make growth-heavy portfolios more vulnerable to a reversal.
